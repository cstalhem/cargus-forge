# Indoor plants

## Light

- [convention] In winter, when daylight is weak, give houseplants full sun, and move many of them closer to south- or west-facing windows [1][2].
- [convention] In summer, strong sun through glass can scorch sensitive plants; bright shade without sharp shadows suits most of them [1].
- [convention] Flowering plants need more light than foliage plants [1].
- [convention] Plants that stand some way into the room get too little light in the Swedish winter. Give them a grow light of about 100 µmol, or about 50 W per m², for 12 to 14 hours a day, on a timer and off at night [3].
- [convention] Plants that tolerate low light include cast iron plant (Aspidistra elatior), ZZ plant (Zamioculcas zamiifolia), Chinese evergreen (Aglaonema), peace lily (Spathiphyllum) and lady palm (Rhapis excelsa); check the ZZ plant, Chinese evergreen and peace lily against the safety section first [4].
- [convention] Weak, spindly growth, pale leaves and poor flowering are signs that a plant gets too little light [2].

## Watering and care

- [convention] Water when the surface of the compost feels dry, not on a fixed schedule. Water until it runs from the drainage holes, then empty the saucer, because roots in soggy compost drown [1].
- [convention] In winter, in a cool room, a plant can usually go a week or two without water [1].
- [convention] Use water at room temperature, and let tap water stand for at least 24 hours before use [1].
- [convention] Give every pot a drainage hole. Repot every two to three years, in spring, into a pot 5 to 10 cm wider [1].
- [convention] Feed from about March to October, and at most once a month in winter [1].
- [convention] Keep plants away from radiators, which dry and brown the leaves, and from cold draughts. Do not leave plants on a windowsill behind drawn curtains on cold nights [1][2].
- [convention] Raise humidity by grouping plants and standing pots on a tray of moist gravel or leca; misting helps only if done often [1].

## Children and pets

- [convention] Giftinformationscentralen says that most children who taste a plant come to little or no harm, but that the sap of some common houseplants can strongly irritate the mouth, throat, eyes and skin [5]. Its strongly irritating plants include prickblad (Dieffenbachia seguine), garderobsblomma or zamiakalla (Zamioculcas zamiifolia), filodendron, High Chaparral and mjölkbuske or gummiplanta (Synadenium grantii) [5][6].
- [convention] Giftinformationscentralen classes nerium (Nerium oleander) and klänglilja as very poisonous, and ökenros (Adenium obesum) as poisonous and able to affect the heart; choose other plants where small children live [6].
- [convention] Giftinformationscentralen lists as irritating, among others: monstera, gullranka (Epipremnum pinnatum), fredskalla (Spathiphyllum), silverkalla (Aglaonema), julstjärna, azalea, cyklamen and hortensia [6].
- [convention] Giftinformationscentralen lists as harmless, among others: svärmors tunga (Sansevieria trifasciata), dracena, fönsterfikus, parasollpilea, peperomia, pelargon, orkidé, julkaktus, novemberkaktus, spjutbräken (Nephrolepis exaltata), flitiga Lisa and hibiskus [6].
- [convention] If a child may have eaten a poisonous plant, call Giftinformationscentralen on 010-456 6700; for life-threatening symptoms, call 112 [5].
- [convention] Lilies (Lilium) are toxic to cats and cause kidney failure [7]. Every part is dangerous, including the pollen a cat grooms off its fur and the water in the vase, so keep no lilies, potted or cut, in a home with a cat, and call a vet at once after contact [8].
- [convention] Spider plant (Chlorophytum comosum) is non-toxic to cats and dogs [9]. Check any other plant by its botanical name in the ASPCA plant list before it goes into a home with pets; eating any plant can make a cat or dog vomit [10].

## Plants and indoor air

- [convention] Choose plants for their looks and for well-being, not to clean the air. A review of 30 years of studies found that matching the air exchange of a building's ventilation, or of a couple of open windows, would take about 100 to 1,000 plants per m² of floor [11].

## Placing plants

- [taste] One large floor plant as a focal point; a group of smaller plants at varied heights; trailing plants on a high shelf or in a hanging pot.
- [taste] Pots that match each other in one material or colour; mixed pots tied together by the room's palette.
