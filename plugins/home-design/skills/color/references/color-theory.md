# Colour theory

## Naming a colour
- [standard] Name a colour by its NCS code, the reference system for colour designation in Sweden. In NCS S 1040-R20B, 10 is the blackness, 40 the chromaticness, and R20B the hue: a red with 20 % blue [1][2]
- [standard] NCS describes every colour by how much it resembles six elementary colours: white, black, yellow, red, blue and green. Whites and greys have no hue, and their code ends in -N [1]
- [convention] Two traditional Swedish whites: målarvit, a broken white that leans towards grey and has long been the most used colour on interior walls, and listvit, a warm white (NCS S 0502-Y) used on factory-painted mouldings and interior doors [7]

## Colour schemes
- [convention] Monochromatic: tints and shades of one hue. It gives a calm, quiet room [3]
- [convention] Analogous: three hues that sit next to each other on the colour wheel, such as blue, blue-green and green. They work together without strong contrast [3]
- [convention] Complementary: two hues opposite each other on the wheel. The contrast is strong, which suits a bold room [3]
- [convention] Split-complementary: three hues that form a narrow triangle on the wheel, one hue and the two neighbours of its opposite. It is more varied but less vibrant than a complementary pair [3]
- [convention] Triadic: three hues spaced evenly around the wheel [3]
- [taste] A quiet room: a monochromatic or analogous scheme; a lively room: a complementary or triadic scheme with one hue clearly in the lead

## Proportions
- [convention] The 60-30-10 split: about 60 % of the room in a dominant colour (mainly the walls, often large furniture), 30 % in a second colour (furniture, curtains, rugs, bedding or one accent wall), and 10 % in an accent colour (cushions, lamps, shades, small furniture) [4]
- [convention] The split is a guide to balance, not a measurement. Use several shades of the dominant colour rather than exactly three colours [4]

## Warm, cool, light and dark
- [convention] Warm colours make a room feel cosier and more intimate; cool colours make it feel calm, open and more spacious [3]
- [convention] Light colours reflect light and make walls recede, so a small room feels larger. Dark colours absorb light and bring the walls in, so a room feels smaller and more intimate, which suits a living room, library or bedroom [3]
- [convention] Muted, natural colours give a soft and calm mood; strong, saturated colours make a room feel energetic [7]
- [taste] A small room: light colours to open it up; or one dark colour on walls and ceiling to make it intimate on purpose

## Light direction and daylight
- [convention] A north-facing room gets little direct sun, so its daylight looks cool, with a blue or grey cast [5][6]
- [taste] In a north-facing room: warm colours with a red or yellow base, such as taupe or warm linen white, to offset the cool light [6]; or cool, calm colours such as white, light grey, blue, mint and green to keep it fresh [5]
- [convention] A south-facing room gets plenty of direct light, which looks warm and yellow, most of all at midday [6]
- [taste] In a south-facing room: cool colours such as dusty blue, mineral grey or sea green to balance the warmth; or whites with a grey or black undertone, so they do not look yellow [6]
- [convention] Colours look clear in a bright room and slightly subdued in a darker one. Allow for how much daylight the room gets in each season, not only on the day you choose [7]
- [convention] Warm, yellow-based whites look warmer in bright daylight, and more intense at night under lamps; cool whites look crisp in daylight [8]

## Testing a colour
- [convention] Make the final choice in the room itself, in its own daylight and lamplight and next to its furnishings, after a test patch: the direction, amount and colour of the light all change the result [7][11]
- [convention] Paint two coats on a large card, about A2, with an unpainted border, and move it between walls and rooms and look at it at different times of day [8]

## Contrast for low vision
- [standard] Where someone in the home has low vision, give a door (its leaf or frame) and the edges of the bottom step and top landing of a stair a lightness contrast of at least 0.40 on the NCS lightness scale against the surface around them. This is the value in Boverket's general advice in the former building rules BBR (BFS 2011:6, sections 3:1423 and 8:232) and in Handisam's accessibility guidance on doors [9][10]
- [standard] Inside a private home the 0.40 contrast is advice, not a requirement. BBR's advice on contrast-marked stairs left out small houses and stairs inside a flat, and Boverket's current accessibility regulations (BFS 2024:12), which replaced BBR's, require that people with reduced orientation ability can find their way but give no contrast value [9][12][13]
- [convention] Make a door handle contrast with the door leaf, so it is easy to find, and check each contrast marking from both directions and in different light [10]
