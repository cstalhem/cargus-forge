# Paint

## Light reflectance value (LRV)
- [standard] LRV is the share of visible light that a surface reflects, weighted by how sensitive the eye is to each part of the light. BS 8493 is the test method for it [1]
- [convention] LRV runs from 0, a surface that absorbs all light, to 100, one that reflects all of it. In practice the whitest paints reach about 94 and the darkest blacks about 2 [2]
- [standard] For the main surfaces of a room, EN 12464-1, the European standard for workplace lighting, gives reflectances of 0.7 to 0.9 for the ceiling, 0.5 to 0.8 for the walls and 0.2 to 0.4 for the floor, that is LRV 70 to 90, 50 to 80 and 20 to 40. It is written for workplaces, so at home it is a target, not a requirement [3]
- [convention] Wall colours by LRV: about 62 to 75 for an average room, 72 or more for a dark room, and 2 to 50 for a feature wall [2]
- [convention] A white with a very high LRV can look stark in a bright room; an off-white, about LRV 73 to 81, softens it [2]
- [convention] A paint's LRV does not change, but how light it looks depends on the light the wall gets: a high-LRV paint on a dim stairwell can look darker than a lower-LRV paint in a bright room. No paint makes up for too little daylight or lamplight [2]
- [convention] Read a paint's LRV from the maker's colour chart, fan deck or the back of the sample card, rather than estimating it [2]
- [standard] The bigger the LRV difference between two surfaces next to each other, the easier it is for a person with low vision to tell them apart. Swedish guidance gives this contrast on the NCS lightness scale instead; see `color-theory.md` [1]

## Sheen
- [standard] EN 13300 sorts interior wall and ceiling paints into four gloss grades, G1 glossy, G2 medium gloss, G3 matt and G4 dull matt, measured at 60° and 85° [4]
- [convention] Swedish paint is sold by glansgrad: helmatt, matt, halvmatt, halvblank, blank and högblank, measured at 60°. The number in a product's name is its gloss, so a "7" is matt and a "20" halvmatt [5]
- [convention] Glossier paint is easier to keep clean; matter paint gives a calmer look [5]
- [convention] Ceilings: a light colour in helmatt paint [5]
- [convention] Walls: matt or helmatt in a living room or bedroom, halvmatt in a kitchen or hall [5]
- [convention] Window frames and interior doors: a joinery paint in halvmatt (20), halvblank (40) or blank (80) [5]
- [taste] Living-room walls: helmatt for a soft, chalky look; matt for a wall you can wipe that still has almost no shine; halvmatt where hands and bags touch the wall often

## Wear and coverage
- [standard] EN 13300 rates a paint's wet scrub resistance from class 1, the most resistant, to class 5, the least [4]
- [convention] For walls that get wiped, such as a hall, kitchen or child's room, choose wet scrub class 1 or 2: a paint of class 3 or worse may not be declared cleanable [4]
- [standard] EN 13300 rates hiding power in four classes that depend on the paint's spreading rate in m²/l, from class 1, which covers best, to class 4 [4]
- [convention] Litres of paint for one coat = wall area in m² ÷ the spreading rate in m²/l on the tin. Measure the whole wall without taking off doors and windows, so the paint does not run out halfway [5]

## Wet rooms
- [standard] In a bathroom, the painting trade's wet-room requirements (MVK) put the walls from floor to ceiling at the bath or shower, and at least 1 m beyond them, in wet zone 1. Paint there must be an approved waterproof system, class VT [6][7]
- [standard] The other bathroom walls, laundry-room walls and the ceiling of every wet room are wet zone 2, which needs at least a water-repellent system, class VA [6][7]
- [standard] A painted wet-room system is waterproofing work: refer it to an MVK-authorised painting company, which uses an approved system, and give no DIY steps [6][7]
