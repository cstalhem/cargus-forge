# Growing zones

Sweden's growing zones (odlingszoner) come from Riksförbundet Svensk Trädgård's zone map. They are a long-standing convention, not a standard or a regulation, so every guideline here is a `convention`.

## The zone map
- [convention] Sweden is divided into eight growing zones (odlingszoner), I to VIII. Zone I has the most favourable growing conditions and zone VIII the harshest [1].
- [convention] The zone map covers woody plants: fruit trees, ornamental trees and shrubs. It does not cover perennials, whose winter survival depends more on the site than on where in the country they grow [2][3].
- [convention] The zones are a rough division. They use isotherms and the length of the growing season (days with a mean temperature of at least +3 °C), and do not account for air humidity [1].
- [convention] Zone ratings change over time: many woody plants' northern limits have moved north, and ratings are revised as trial results come in [1].
- [convention] The user finds their zone on Svensk Trädgård's digital zone map by typing in their address [4]. When the user gives only a town, an estimated zone is an estimate: say so, and suggest checking the map.

## Reading a plant's zone rating
- [convention] A higher zone number on a plant label means a hardier plant. A plant rated for a zone also grows in every milder zone, so a plant rated "zon I–IV" suits zones I, II, III and IV [5].
- [convention] Where the rating is in doubt, SLU's plant database gives the lower (milder) zone [5].
- [convention] Ratings vary between catalogues and books, and many woody plants do not follow them; treat a rating as a guide, not a guarantee [6].
- [convention] Labels from other countries use other zone systems. As a rough, conservative rule for northern Sweden, subtract one from a Norwegian zone (Norwegian zone 5 is about Swedish zone IV) and add one to a Finnish zone (Finnish zone 4 is about Swedish zone V). USDA and European zones do not translate well to Nordic conditions [6].
- [convention] A zone rating assumes the plant's basic site needs are met; a plant that needs shelter is rated on the assumption that it is not placed in drying wind and strong sun [5].

## Microclimate
- [convention] Within one zone the local climate can differ by the equivalent of one to two zones, so judge the actual planting spot [5].
- [convention] Sun, wind, slope, soil and nearby rocks, walls and water can shift conditions by up to a full zone [1].
- [convention] City sites usually grow better than rural sites in the same zone, because paving and buildings store heat and release it at night [1].
- [convention] Coasts and large lakes give higher air humidity, so some evergreens, such as ivy (murgröna), grow higher there than inland in the same zone [1].
- [convention] Altitude shortens the growing season: a Norwegian estimate is five to six fewer days for every 100 m of height [1].
- [convention] Snow cover protects roots, which tolerate less cold than the parts above ground [1].
- [convention] Changeable winters with warm spells can be harder on some plants than cold, stable winters, because plants wake too early and then get frost damage [2].
- [convention] Evergreens such as yew and rhododendron can dry out in late winter when sun warms the leaves while the ground is frozen; the damage is worst in sunny, windy spots [2].
