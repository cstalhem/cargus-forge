# Planting

## Choosing plants
- [convention] Let the site choose the plant: match sun or shade and dry or moist ground before you pick a species [1].
- [convention] Check that a tree or shrub survives winter in the user's growing zone (see `growing-zones.md`) before you suggest it [1].
- [convention] E-planta is a quality label for Swedish-grown trees and shrubs that are tested for Swedish conditions; prefer it when the user buys woody plants [1][4].
- [convention] Apple trees need another apple variety nearby to be pollinated [1].

## Hardy trees and shrubs for northern zones
- [convention] In SLU Movium's survey of growers' experience in northern Sweden, these common species were reported hardy as far as zone VII or VIII: Siberian larch (Larix sibirica), Siberian fir (Abies sibirica), alpine currant / måbär (Ribes alpinum), cinnamon rose / kanelros (Rosa majalis), woolly willow / ullvide (Salix lanata), birch-leaved spirea / björkspirea (Spiraea betulifolia) and Hungarian lilac / ungersk syren (Syringa josikaea) [3].
- [convention] In the same survey, panicle hydrangea / vipphortensia (Hydrangea paniculata), Serbian spruce (Picea omorika), small-leaved lime / skogslind (Tilia cordata) and guelder rose / olvon (Viburnum opulus) were reported mainly in zones V and VI [3].
- [convention] A plant at the edge of its hardiness can behave differently: smoke bush (Cotinus coggygria) acts as a half-perennial that partly freezes back and regrows [3].

## Flowers for pollinators
- [convention] Mix trees and shrubs with different flowering times, and add early and late bloomers, because most flowering on the ground is in June and July. Early: Cornelian cherry / körsbärskornell, mezereon / tibast, hazel / hassel and willows. Late: shrubby cinquefoil / tok [4].
- [convention] Choose single flowers: some heavily bred varieties with double flowers, such as some hydrangeas and shrub roses, have no pollen or nectar [4].
- [convention] Keep goat willow / sälg: it is vital for insects that wake early in spring, and about 15 wild bee species depend on it for pollen [4].
- [convention] A rule of thumb is "varannan vild": at least every other new tree should be a kind that wild pollinators can use, such as a garden form of a native tree like crab apple / bukettapel or ornamental cherry [4].
- [convention] Pollen- and nectar-rich shrubs with no known invasive risk include currants, raspberry, slån, hawthorn / hagtorn, single fragrant roses (not vresros), lavender, heather / ljung, rowan / rönn and elder / fläder [4].
- [convention] Leave fallen leaves in hedges and beds in autumn: hedgehogs and many insects need them to overwinter [5].

## Pots and balconies
- [convention] For a tree or shrub in a pot, choose one rated at least one zone hardier than the user's zone, because the cold reaches the root ball directly [6].
- [convention] A pot for a larger shrub or tree should hold about 50 litres of soil and be about 60 cm across; bigger is better [6].
- [convention] Every pot needs a drainage hole, and feet under it help water run off. Choose frost-proof concrete, zinc or rubber; ordinary clay pots absorb water and handle frost poorly [6].
- [convention] Over winter, move potted trees under a roof before the heavy autumn rain, bury the pot in the ground or a compost heap or insulate it to protect the roots, and water sparingly while it is above freezing [6].
- [convention] On a balcony or terrace, use generous pots, fill with a potting or garden soil over a layer of about 10 cm of expanded clay (lecakulor), keep the soil moist, and water early in the morning or in the evening on hot days [7].
- [taste] Balcony perennials: lavender, coral bells / alunrot, moss phlox / mossflox, Japanese blood grass; seasonal flowers: pelargonium, snapdragon / lejongap, French lavender / skärmlavendel; a structural shrub or tree: panicle hydrangea or a cut-leaf Japanese maple [7].

## Preparing for winter
- [convention] Give perennial plants no nitrogen fertiliser after July; phosphorus and potassium at the end of July can help them overwinter [2].
- [convention] Plant sensitive plants in a warm, well-drained spot and reduce watering from mid-August, so they finish growing before winter [2].

## Invasive species
- [standard] Plants on the EU list of invasive alien species may not be imported, sold, exchanged, grown, transported, used or kept alive (Regulation (EU) 1143/2014). Garden-relevant ones include Japanese knotweed / parkslide, giant and hybrid knotweed / jätteslide and hybridslide, Himalayan balsam / jättebalsamin, giant hogweed / jätteloka, American skunk cabbage / gul skunkkalla, common milkweed / sidenört, fountain grass / fjäderborstgräs (Cenchrus setaceus), tree of heaven / gudaträd and Japanese hop / japansk humle [8].
- [standard] Sweden's national list of invasive alien species applies from 15 May 2026. Its nine land plants are blomsterlupin, sandlupin, vresros, kaukasiskt fetblad, sibiriskt fetblad, strandkotula, spärroxbär, kanadensiskt gullris and höstgullris; it is forbidden to import, grow, breed, transport, sell or release them [9].
- [standard] Listed plants already on a property may stay but may not be actively cultivated, and the owner must take the steps needed to stop them spreading from the property [10].
- [convention] To stop spread, cut the plants or flowers before they set seed, or dig up small patches; put the waste in a well-sealed bag with the combustible waste [10].

## Poisonous plants
- [convention] Where children play, avoid the most dangerous plants. Very poisonous, even in small amounts: foxglove / fingerborgsblomma, oleander / nerium, cowbane / sprängört, monkshood / stormhatt and angel's trumpet / änglatrumpet. Poisonous but rarely serious: laburnum / gullregn, yew / idegran, lily of the valley / liljekonvalj and mezereon / tibast [11].
- [convention] If a child may have eaten a poisonous plant, call Giftinformationscentralen on 010-456 67 00, or 112 in an emergency [11].
